Fox & Hedgehog Derby"The fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing" — Archilochus, via Berlin (1953)

Introduction

Between 1984 and 2003, Philip Tetlock collected roughly 28,000 forecasts from 284 experts. In aggregate, expert political forecasting was close to chance. But the aggregate concealed a split: performance divided not by ideology, education or access, but by cognitive style. Hedgehogs — one big theory, applied everywhere, expressed confidently — lost to foxes, who stitched together many partial models and revised freely. This derby lets you race both.

You will forecast twelve questions about a fictional region, Veridia (so no real-world knowledge can help you), alongside a Hedgehog bot and a Fox bot; accumulate (or decline) television bookings; read the Soviet-collapse natural experiment; and finally assemble a forecasting team.

All randomness is seeded; identical seeds reproduce identical runs.

Module C — Collapse of the Wall

A natural experiment, 1989–91. Three contemporaneous readings of the Soviet system. For each, predict whether it anticipated the timing and the mechanism of the collapse, then reveal. (Two to three minutes; context, not a game.)

Reading 1 of 3 — the committed-Marxist hedgehog

"The Soviet system commands the loyalty of its cadres and the machinery of a superpower. Reform will strengthen, not dissolve, socialist rule; the fundamentals are stable." One big theory: the system's ideological core guarantees its persistence.

Reading 2 of 3 — the single-cause free-market hedgehog

"Command economies are doomed; collapse is inevitable and imminent." One big theory again — and this reading had predicted imminent collapse continuously since at least 1980.

Debrief

The pedagogical point

Three findings, in ascending order of maturity. First: aggregate expert forecasting in Tetlock's twenty-year sample was close to chance — the dart-throwing chimp is not a joke but a baseline, and the Hedgehog bot fails to clear it on a non-trivial share of seeded runs here, exactly as in the data. Second: confident single-model forecasting earns fame and loses accuracy — the scatter you produced is Tetlock's negative fame–accuracy correlation in miniature. Third, and least comfortable for fox-triumphalism: the finding is not "foxes good, hedgehogs bad". Hedgehogs are often better at generating the bold, testable hypotheses that give a team something to judge; foxes are better at judging and revising them. Module D's production function — where all-fox and all-hedgehog teams both underperform mixed ones — is the mature lesson: structure teams for complementarity.
